Buying property in Turkey as a foreigner: legal checks, the title deed transfer and what to do if something goes wrong

Dr Alihan Sağır, Turkish lawyer (Avukat), Antalya Bar Association · Prepared: 9 November 2026

1. Most foreigners can own property in Turkey, within the limits of the Land Registry Act (No. 2644, Art. 35): a cap per person nationwide, a cap per district, and restrictions in military and security zones. 2. Start with the title deed record (tapu). It shows the owner, mortgages, seizures and other annotations, and whether the flat is registered as a completed unit (kat mülkiyeti) or only as a right to a future flat (kat irtifakı). 3. Check the building too: occupancy permit (yapı kullanma izin belgesi), zoning, service-charge debts and unpaid property tax. 4. Ownership passes only at the land registry. A reservation paper or deposit does not transfer anything. 5. At the registry you will need a valid passport, a photograph, a sworn translator if you do not speak Turkish, compulsory earthquake insurance (DASK) and proof of payment. 6. Do not under-declare the price. It affects fees, taxes and your rights in a later dispute. 7. A home does not by itself give residence or citizenship. 8. If something goes wrong, the remedy depends on the problem, and some routes need mediation or have short deadlines.

In short

Can a foreigner own property in Turkey?

Generally, yes. Under Art. 35 of the Land Registry Act, foreign individuals who are nationals of the countries determined by the President may acquire immovable property and limited rights over it. The land registry authority (TKGM) states that the list in force covers nationals of 185 countries. The earlier requirement of reciprocity between Turkey and the buyer's country was removed in 2012.

The same article sets limits that matter in practice:

  • Per district: the total area held by foreign individuals may not exceed 10% of the privately owned surface area of a district.
  • Per person: no more than 30 hectares nationwide. The President may raise this limit up to twice that figure.
  • Military and security zones: under the Military Restricted Zones and Security Zones Act (No. 2565), purchases by foreigners in these areas are prohibited or need special permission. The registry holds the zone information on its own records and checks it on application.
  • Undeveloped land: foreigners who buy land with no building on it must submit a development project to the competent ministry within two years.

The registry checks these limits on every application.

What should you check before you buy?

A lawyer in Turkey can review the following before you pay.

1. The title deed record. Ask for the current record. It shows the registered owner (and whether the seller is that person), the type of property, the share of the land, and the annotations. Look for:

  • a mortgage (ipotek), which must be cleared or taken over;
  • a seizure (haciz) or precautionary injunction, which usually means a creditor or a court case;
  • other annotations such as a sale promise, a right of pre-emption, a lease or a pending lawsuit.

2. Completed flat or right to a future flat? In Turkey the registry distinguishes between:

  • Flat ownership (kat mülkiyeti): the building is finished and has its occupancy permit, and each flat is registered as a separate unit.
  • Floor easement (kat irtifakı): the building is unfinished or has no occupancy permit yet, and the buyer is registered as having a right to a future flat. Under the Condominium Act, a floor easement is converted into flat ownership on the basis of the occupancy permit for the whole building.

A flat registered only as kat irtifakı can be a legitimate purchase, particularly off-plan, but it needs more care: utilities, mortgages and resale can all be affected until the occupancy permit exists.

3. Zoning and permits. Confirm with the municipality that the building has a construction licence and occupancy permit matching the approved plans, and that there is no demolition order or urban-transformation decision. Extra floors and enclosed balconies are common problems.

4. Debts attached to the flat. Under the Condominium Act (No. 634, Art. 20), each owner pays a share of the building's running costs, and late payment carries monthly compensation of 5%. Ask the site management for a written statement that no service charges are outstanding. Check property tax at the municipality, and make sure utility accounts (electricity, water, gas, internet) are settled and can be transferred to your name.

5. The seller's authority. Check that the seller is the owner or properly authorised, and who must sign where the property belongs to a couple, heirs or a company.

Is a valuation report required?

For an ordinary purchase, no. A valuation report is not currently required when a foreigner buys a home in Turkey. For a purchase made to obtain Turkish citizenship, yes: the land registry (TKGM) requires a report from a valuation firm authorised by the Capital Markets Board (SPK), and an amount determination certificate (tutar tespit belgesi) is produced from it. The rule for ordinary purchases has changed several times since 2019, so confirm it with the land registry office when you buy. An independent valuation can still be worth having before paying a price.

How does the title deed transfer work?

The transfer takes place at the district land registry office (tapu müdürlüğü) for the place where the property is located: in Antalya province, for example, Muratpaşa, Konyaaltı, Alanya, Manavgat (Side) or Kemer. Typical steps:

  1. Appointment. Through the Alo 181 call centre or the e-appointment and Web Tapu systems (the online services need a foreigner identification number), or in person at the office.
  2. Identity. A valid passport or national identity document; expired documents are not accepted. The registry takes one photograph for the identity declaration form and, if you have no foreigner identification number, arranges one.
  3. Sworn translator. If a party does not speak Turkish, a sworn translator authorised by the regional justice commission must attend.
  4. Earthquake insurance. A compulsory earthquake insurance policy (DASK) is required for flats and houses.
  5. Proof of payment. A foreign buyer is generally required to sell the purchase price in foreign currency to a bank and to give the registry the foreign currency purchase certificate (döviz alım belgesi). TKGM publishes rules on when a bank transfer receipt may be used instead. Take advice before sending the money.
  6. Fees. The title deed fee under the Fees Act (No. 492, Tariff No. 4) is 2% (20 per thousand) of the declared price, paid separately by buyer and seller, and not less than the property's tax value. The registry also charges a fixed service fee. Rates can change, so confirm them before you sign.
  7. Signing. Both parties sign before the registry officer, and the buyer is registered as owner.

What if you cannot be in Turkey?

You can buy through a representative with a power of attorney (vekaletname), signed either at a Turkish consulate abroad, or before a notary in your own country with an apostille (Turkey and most countries relevant to this readership are parties to the 1961 Hague Convention) and then translated into Turkish by a sworn translator. The registry requires the original or a certified copy, with translation. The document should name the property and the powers given, including the price and registration; a defective document is a common reason for delay. A lawyer in Turkey can supply the wording before you sign abroad.

Paying safely

  • A reservation deposit is not a transfer. A contract to sell real estate is valid only in official form (Code of Obligations, Art. 237; Civil Code, Art. 706). An agency "reservation" paper does not transfer ownership, and money paid under it is exposed if the seller changes their mind, has creditors or sells to someone else. A sale promise should be made before a notary and can be annotated on the title deed.
  • Pay by bank, against registration, with proof that matches the contract.
  • The deed should show the real price. Fees are calculated on the declared price, and the Fees Act (Art. 63) lets the authorities charge the difference, with a penalty, if the declared price does not reflect the real one. Money paid above the declared price is also hard to prove in a later dispute. Take advice on your home-country position too.

Buying off-plan (before the building is finished)

Many purchases on the Antalya coast are made from a developer while the building is under construction. Where the buyer is a consumer, the Consumer Protection Act (No. 6502, Arts. 40–46) governs this "pre-paid housing sale" (ön ödemeli konut satışı):

  • The developer cannot contract with consumers before obtaining the construction licence, and must give a pre-contract information form at least one day before (Art. 40).
  • The sale must be registered at the land registry or, for a sale promise, made before a notary; the seller may not take any payment before a valid contract exists (Art. 41).
  • Above a size set by the ministry, the seller must provide building-completion insurance or other security (Art. 42).
  • The consumer has 14 days to withdraw without giving a reason (Art. 43).
  • Delivery must be within the contractual period and in any case within 48 months of the contract (Art. 44).
  • The consumer may withdraw from the contract for up to 24 months, with limited compensation to the seller. If the seller fails to perform, the seller cannot claim any amount (Art. 45).

Whether the Act applies depends on the facts, in particular whether the buyer acts as a consumer and the seller as a business. Check the contract, the licence and the developer's security before paying.

Short-term letting

The Act on Tourism Letting of Residences (No. 7464) requires a permit (izin belgesi) from the Ministry of Culture and Tourism before a residence is let for tourism, meaning lettings of up to 100 days. The application requires the unanimous approval of all owners in the building (Art. 3), and fines apply to unpermitted letting. In a mixed building this can be hard to obtain, so check the management plan before buying a flat for holiday letting.

Residence and citizenship

Residence. Owning a home gives no automatic right to stay beyond the period allowed for your nationality. A short-term residence permit can be granted to foreigners who own property in Turkey (Law No. 6458, Art. 31); the Ministry sets the type and value of property that qualifies. The Ministry of Interior's Presidency of Migration Management closes some neighbourhoods to new residence permit registrations where the share of foreign residents is high, and publishes the list. In Antalya, the official list has included Liman, Hurma and Sarısu in Konyaaltı. The list is revised from time to time, so check the current version with the provincial migration office (İl Göç İdaresi) or on goc.gov.tr before buying in order to live at a particular address.

Citizenship. A foreigner who buys property worth at least USD 400,000 (or the equivalent) may apply for citizenship if a note is placed on the title deed that the property will not be sold for three years (Citizenship Regulation, Art. 20). The value is confirmed by an SPK-authorised valuation report and the registry's amount determination certificate; co-owned shares do not qualify, according to TKGM. The conditions are set by regulation and have changed before, so check them when you buy.

If you inherit property instead

If you hold property in Turkey and die, or you are an heir, the rules are different. See the guide Inherited property in Turkey.

What if something goes wrong?

The registration is wrong or the seller had no right to sell. The Civil Code allows an action to correct the land register where a right was registered without a valid basis (Art. 1025; commonly called tapu iptal ve tescil). A buyer who relied in good faith on the register is protected (Art. 1023); someone who knew or should have known of the problem is not (Art. 1024). These actions go to the court where the property is located, which has exclusive jurisdiction (Code of Civil Procedure, Art. 12). The State is liable for damage caused by keeping the register (Civil Code, Art. 1007).

The home is defective. The remedies depend on who sold it.

  • Developer or other business seller (consumer sale). The Consumer Protection Act applies. The buyer may withdraw, ask for a reduction in price, free repair or replacement (Art. 11). Liability lasts for 5 years from delivery for homes and holiday properties, and the period does not apply if the seller concealed the defect through gross negligence or fraud (Art. 12).
  • Private seller. The Code of Obligations applies: the seller is liable for material, legal or economic defects even if unaware of them (Art. 219). The buyer must inspect the property and notify the seller promptly once a defect is found (Art. 223). Claims for defects in a building are time-barred five years after ownership passes, or twenty years if the seller was grossly at fault (Art. 244). Because the duty to notify is strict, notify the seller in writing and take advice quickly.

Forum and mediation. For 2026, consumer disputes valued below TRY 186,000 go to the consumer arbitration committee and larger ones to the consumer court (Ministry of Trade notice, Official Gazette 23 December 2025). Before a consumer-court claim, applying to a mediator is a precondition (Consumer Protection Act, Art. 73/A), with exceptions that include disputes arising from rights in rem over immovable property. For commercial claims for money, mediation is also a precondition (Commercial Code, Art. 5/A). A claim filed without required mediation is dismissed on procedural grounds, so identify the route before suing.

Disputes with the building management. Disputes among flat owners and with the manager are decided by the court of peace (sulh hukuk mahkemesi) at the location of the building. An owner who disagrees with a decision of the owners' meeting must apply to cancel it within one month of the decision (or of learning of it), and in any event within six months (Condominium Act, Art. 33). Unpaid service charges can be recovered by enforcement proceedings or a claim.

Frequently asked questions

Can a foreigner own property in Turkey?

Yes. Nationals of most countries can buy, subject to the 10% district and 30 hectare national limits and the restrictions in military and security zones.

Do I need a lawyer to buy a house in Turkey?

The law does not require one. A lawyer in Turkey can review the title deed record, the permits and the contract, and can act for you under a power of attorney.

Can I buy without travelling to Turkey?

Yes, by a power of attorney signed at a Turkish consulate, or before a notary and apostilled, then translated into Turkish.

What are the title deed fees?

2% of the declared price from the buyer and 2% from the seller, plus a fixed service fee, under the current rules. Confirm with the registry before you sign.

Does buying a home give me residence or citizenship?

Not automatically. A short-term residence permit may be available, though some neighbourhoods are closed to new applications. Citizenship by investment requires property worth at least USD 400,000 and a three-year note on the deed.

Can I let my flat to holidaymakers?

Only with a permit under Law No. 7464, which requires the approval of all owners in the building.
