Medical malpractice in Turkey: criminal and public routes

Dr Alihan Sağır, Turkish lawyer (Avukat), Antalya Bar Association · Prepared: 19 November 2026

When treatment in Turkey goes wrong, compensation from a private clinic is only one of the possible routes. A criminal complaint, a complaint to the health authorities and, for public hospitals, a claim against the State follow different rules and different deadlines. This guide explains those routes in plain English, for patients living in the UK or elsewhere.

In short

  1. Negligent injury and negligent death are crimes. Turkish Criminal Code (TCK) Art. 89 covers negligent injury and Art. 85 negligent death.
  2. A complaint is required for negligent injury. Investigation and prosecution of negligent injury depend on the victim's complaint (Art. 89(5)). Negligent death is not subject to this rule.
  3. The complaint deadline is six months. It starts on the day the complainant learns of the act and of who committed it (TCK Art. 73).
  4. A complaint is made to the public prosecutor or the police or gendarmerie, in writing or orally to be recorded (Criminal Procedure Code, Art. 158). A lawyer can file it.
  5. Doctors and dentists have a special investigation procedure. For medical procedures, a permission of a Ministry of Health board is needed before a prosecutor can investigate (Law 3359, additional Art. 18).
  6. The criminal case does not pay compensation by itself. A civil claim is a separate track (see the private clinic guide).
  7. Administrative complaints are separate again. The Ministry of Health, through SABİM, CİMER or the provincial health directorate, can look at licensing and authorisation issues. It does not award damages.
  8. For public hospitals, the claim is against the administration, not the doctor personally (Constitution Art. 129), and a prior application and strict deadlines apply (Administrative Procedure Law, Arts. 12 and 13).

Criminal complaint: negligent injury and negligent death

Under the Turkish Criminal Code (Law No. 5237), a person who by negligence causes pain, or harms another person's health or perception, is punished under Art. 89(1). The penalty is increased where the injury has certain serious results, such as a broken bone, a permanent weakening of an organ, a permanent facial scar or a life-threatening condition (Art. 89(2)), and increased further for results such as loss of an organ's function or permanent change of the face (Art. 89(3)). Where negligence causes the death of a person, Art. 85 applies.

In a medical case, the question is whether the practitioner breached the care required in the circumstances, and whether that breach caused the harm. The prosecutor will normally ask for expert evidence, often from the Forensic Medicine Institution (Adli Tıp Kurumu) or a university board. The criminal file is therefore built on the medical record, so the records, images and consent forms matter from the first day.

Is a complaint required? For negligent injury, yes. Art. 89(5) provides that investigation and prosecution of negligent injury depend on a complaint. The same paragraph provides that, apart from the injury in the first paragraph, no complaint is required where the offence is committed by conscious negligence (bilinçli taksir). In practice this means that a complaint should be filed in any case where injury is alleged, and the prosecutor decides on the legal classification.

The six-month limit. TCK Art. 73 provides that if the person entitled to complain does not do so within six months, investigation and prosecution cannot be carried out. The period starts when the person learns of the act and of who committed it. A patient who only discovers the result later, for example after a second opinion at home, may count from that date, but the date of discovery will be examined, so it should be recorded in writing.

Withdrawal. In offences that depend on a complaint, withdrawal of the complaint by the injured person ends the prosecution (Art. 73(4)). This is relevant if the parties later reach a settlement.

How the complaint is filed

The Criminal Procedure Code (Law No. 5271), Art. 158, provides that a complaint can be made to the public prosecutor's office or to law enforcement. It can be written, or oral and then recorded. A complaint sent to the governor, district governor or a court is forwarded to the prosecutor.

A patient who has returned to the UK does not have to travel. A lawyer in Turkey can prepare and file the complaint with the prosecutor of the place where the treatment took place, and attach the medical documents. The details of the power of attorney (notarisation and legalisation abroad) depend on the case and are better checked with the Turkish lawyer before signing.

Investigation permission. Medical staff are treated differently from other suspects. Under Law 3359 on Health Services, additional Art. 18, investigations about the examination, diagnosis and treatment carried out by doctors, dentists and other health professionals in public or private health institutions and foundation universities are conducted under Law 4483 on the Prosecution of Civil Servants. The permission to investigate is given by the Professional Liability Board (Mesleki Sorumluluk Kurulu) within the Ministry of Health. The Board may have a preliminary examination made by officials of the provincial health directorate for private institutions. The time limits in Art. 7 of Law 4483 are doubled for this procedure, and the Board's decisions can be appealed to the Ankara Regional Administrative Court. The practical effect is that a complaint about a medical procedure is not examined by the prosecutor alone, and the file can take longer.

The criminal case and the civil claim are separate

A criminal case is brought by the State. The patient takes part as the injured person, and the court does not, as a rule, award the patient a refund or the cost of corrective treatment in the criminal judgment. Claims for compensation and refunds follow the civil route (against a private provider) or the administrative route (against a public hospital). A criminal complaint and a civil or administrative claim can run in parallel, and each has its own time limits. For civil claims against private clinics and dentists, see claims against private clinics and dentists in Turkey. For the overall map, see medical treatment gone wrong in Turkey.

Complaints to the Ministry of Health

A complaint to the health authorities does not pay compensation, but it can start an inspection of a facility or a person, and it creates a written record.

  • SABİM and CİMER. The Ministry of Health Communication Centre (SABİM) takes applications by telephone on 184, and the Presidency's communication centre (CİMER) takes applications on the national government channel. Provincial health directorate pages describe SABİM and CİMER as the main channels, and applications are directed to the relevant facility, provincial directorate or Ministry unit.
  • Provincial Health Directorate (İl Sağlık Müdürlüğü). Private clinics, dental practices and hospitals are licensed and supervised at provincial level. A written complaint can be filed with the directorate of the province where the facility is located. Provincial pages differ on whether complaints can be sent by post or must be handed in, so the local rules should be checked.

These bodies review the facility's licence, hygiene and conduct. They do not decide who is at fault in a medical dispute and do not order refunds.

Health tourism authorisation

Patients who travel to Turkey for treatment are covered by the International Health Tourism and Tourist Health Regulation. The first regulation, of 2017, was replaced by a new regulation published in the Official Gazette on 26 April 2025 (no. 32882).

Under the 2025 text:

  • Health facilities and intermediary companies working with international patients need an authorisation certificate. For a facility it is issued by the Ministry of Health, and for an intermediary by USHAŞ, the health tourism agency (Art. 5).
  • An intermediary (aracı kuruluş) is a company that advises international patients and their companions on treatment and organises the related services. An authorisation cannot be transferred (Art. 5).
  • The facility and the intermediary are each responsible for the services they provide, depending on the type of service (Art. 6(3)). They must also evaluate complaints and take corrective action (Art. 6(4)).
  • The 2017 regulation was repealed (Art. 17).

This matters for two reasons. First, an agency or facilitator that sold the treatment may be a regulated intermediary, and its authorisation status is a fact that can be raised in a complaint. Second, a complaint to the Ministry can concern the facility's authorisation, not only its medical standard. The regulation does not set out a separate complaint form, so the channels above are the practical route.

Chambers of doctors and dentists

The medical and dental chambers are professional bodies, and a complaint about a practitioner's conduct can be sent to the chamber where the practitioner is registered. The legal framework of professional discipline is currently changing after Constitutional Court decisions, so this is a professional-ethics track with an uncertain procedure, not a route to compensation.

Public hospitals: claims against the administration

If the treatment took place in a state hospital, a university hospital run by the State or another public institution, the position is different. Art. 129 of the Constitution provides that compensation claims arising from faults committed by civil servants and other public officials in the exercise of their powers can be brought only against the administration, with a right of recourse against the official. The patient therefore does not sue the doctor personally in a civil court. The claim is a full remedy action (tam yargı davası) before the administrative courts.

Prior application. Under the Administrative Procedure Law (Law No. 2577), Art. 13, a person whose rights are harmed by an administrative act (as opposed to a formal decision) must first apply to the administration to have the loss made good. The application must be made within one year of learning of the act, and in any case within five years of the act. If the application is rejected in whole or in part, the lawsuit can be filed within the time limit running from the notice of rejection. If the administration does not answer within thirty days, the application is treated as rejected, and the time limit starts when that period ends.

Time to sue. The general time limit for filing an action in the administrative courts is sixty days (Art. 7), counted from the notification of the rejection or, where there is no answer, from the end of the thirty-day period. Art. 12 allows the injured person to file a full remedy action directly, or together with an annulment action, where a formal administrative decision caused the harm. For medical treatment, the usual case falls under Art. 13, because medical treatment is an act, not a decision.

These time limits are strict, and an application sent to the wrong body or after the period can be fatal to the claim. They should be checked against the facts of the case.

Practical points before leaving Turkey or after returning

  • Request the full medical file, imaging, prescriptions, consent forms and invoices, and keep the originals of messages with the clinic and any agency.
  • Photograph the condition and keep any removed items, for example parts of a crown or an implant, if the clinician returns them.
  • Obtain a written report from a doctor at home describing the condition. It is useful as evidence, although the Turkish court will usually appoint its own experts.
  • Write down the date on which you first learned of the injury and of who treated you. This date is relevant to the six-month and one-year limits above.
  • Do not sign a waiver or a settlement document in Turkish without having it translated and reviewed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I make a criminal complaint about a doctor in Turkey from the UK?

Yes, in principle. The complaint is filed with the public prosecutor's office where the treatment took place, and a lawyer in Turkey can file it on your behalf under a power of attorney. The six-month limit in TCK Art. 73 applies, so the date you learned of the harm matters.

How long do I have to report negligent injury in Turkey?

Six months from the day you learned of the act and of who committed it (TCK Art. 73). Negligent injury needs a complaint (Art. 89(5)). The period for a complaint is a legal condition, so it is better to treat the date as fixed rather than flexible.

Does a criminal complaint get me compensation?

Not by itself. A criminal case examines whether an offence was committed and punishes it. Refunds, corrective treatment costs and damages are claimed in a civil action against a private provider, or in an administrative action against a public hospital.

Can I sue a doctor in a state hospital in Turkey?

Not personally in a civil court. Under Art. 129 of the Constitution, compensation claims for the faults of public officials are brought against the administration. The claim goes to the administrative court after a prior application to the administration (Administrative Procedure Law, Art. 13).

Where do I complain about a clinic or an agency that arranged my treatment?

The Ministry of Health channels (SABİM on 184, CİMER and the provincial health directorate) handle complaints about facilities. An agency selling treatment to international patients needs an authorisation under the health tourism regulation, issued by USHAŞ, and operating without it can be reported to the same channels.

Do prosecutors need permission to investigate a doctor?

For medical procedures, yes. Under Law 3359, additional Art. 18, an investigation permission is given by the Professional Liability Board of the Ministry of Health, for doctors and dentists in public and private institutions. The Board's decisions can be appealed to the Ankara Regional Administrative Court.

Will a Turkish criminal conviction help in the UK?

That depends on UK law and on the UK forum, and it is a question for a solicitor in the UK.
