Medical law · Turkish law

Claims against a private clinic or dentist in Turkey

Dr Alihan Sağır, Turkish lawyer (Avukat), Antalya Bar Association · Prepared: 19 November 2026

A British patient who had dental or cosmetic treatment at a private clinic in Turkey, and is left with a poor result, pain or a bill for repair work at home, has several civil routes under Turkish law. The contract, the amount in dispute and the people responsible decide the route. This guide explains the civil track against private providers. For the wider map see Medical or dental treatment gone wrong in Turkey, and for criminal and administrative routes see Medical malpractice in Turkey: criminal complaints and Ministry of Health complaints.

In short

  1. Contract. Turkish law treats treatment as a mandate (vekalet) in principle, but aesthetic and prosthetic work may be seen as a contract for a result (eser). The classification is disputed and fact-specific.
  2. Consumer status. A private patient is usually a consumer under Law No. 6502, which covers mandate and work contracts and does not mention nationality.
  3. Informed consent (aydınlatılmış onam) is a legal duty: benefits, risks and alternatives must be explained.
  4. Forum. In 2026, disputes below 186,000 TL go to the consumer arbitration committee (tüketici hakem heyeti). Higher disputes go to the consumer court (tüketici mahkemesi) after mandatory mediation.
  5. Liability can lie with the clinic, the doctor and sometimes an intermediary, each on a different legal basis.
  6. Heads of loss: refund, corrective treatment, other pecuniary loss (maddi tazminat) and non-pecuniary damages (manevi tazminat).
  7. Time limits run from two to ten years depending on the legal basis.
  8. A court-appointed expert (bilirkişi) normally decides the medical questions, so records and an independent second opinion matter.

The contract between patient and provider

When a patient agrees a treatment with a private clinic or dentist for a fee, a contract arises. The Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098, "TBK") defines a mandate as a contract in which the agent undertakes to handle a task for the principal (Art. 502). The agent must act loyally and with care, and the standard is that of a prudent agent in a similar field (Art. 506). Under this model the provider owes proper, careful treatment but does not promise a cure.

A contract for work (eser sözleşmesi) is one in which the contractor undertakes to produce a work for a price (Art. 470). Turkish writers and courts have, in some cases, treated aesthetic procedures and implant or prosthetic work, such as crowns and bridges, as closer to a contract for a result, while treating the medical part of care as a mandate. Others see a mixed contract. For veneers, crowns, implants or a hair transplant, the treatment plan, the quotation and the messages with the clinic show what was agreed and promised.

The classification matters because it changes the rules. Under a contract for a result the patient can rely on the defect rules for work (Arts. 474 to 478), including the duty to inspect the result and report defects within a suitable time (Arts. 474 and 477). Under a mandate the question is whether the provider breached the duty of care.

Whichever model applies, an advance agreement excluding liability for gross fault is void, and for a service that may be provided only with an official licence so is one excluding liability for slight fault (TBK Art. 115). Standard "no liability" wording in a clinic form therefore does not remove the claim.

Is a patient a consumer?

Law No. 6502 defines a consumer as a natural or legal person acting for non-commercial and non-professional purposes (Art. 3). A consumer transaction covers any contract between a person acting commercially or professionally and a consumer, expressly including mandate and work contracts. The provider is a "service provider" (sağlayıcı). The text does not depend on nationality, so a British patient treated for personal reasons falls within these definitions.

Consumer courts have jurisdiction over such disputes (Art. 73(1)), with the arbitration committee and mediation steps described below.

Practitioners generally take private clinic and dentist disputes to the consumer court. Whether a claim against the individual doctor, based on tort rather than contract, is heard there or in a general civil court is less settled, and the choice should be made with the facts in hand.

Which law and which court?

A foreign patient will ask whether Turkish law applies at all. Under the Turkish private international law statute (Law No. 5718), parties may choose the law of the contract (Art. 24). The special consumer rule that protects a consumer's home-country law does not apply to services that must be provided in a country other than the consumer's home country (Art. 26(4)). In a typical treatment-abroad case, that rule is therefore unlikely to help. For tort claims, the law of the place of the wrongful act applies, with a different rule if the damage arose elsewhere (Art. 34). Clinic and agency forms sometimes contain a choice-of-law or court clause and should be read first.

For local jurisdiction, the general court is that of the defendant's seat (Code of Civil Procedure, Art. 6), and contract claims may also be filed where the contract is to be performed (Art. 10). For a clinic in Antalya, that normally points to Antalya. The consumer-residence venue in Law No. 6502 Art. 73(5) is of limited use to a patient living in the UK.

Informed consent and the duty of care

The Patient Rights Regulation (1998) gives the patient the right to ask about their health, the planned procedures, the benefits and likely drawbacks, the alternatives and the consequences of refusing treatment. It states that medical interventions require the patient's consent, that no one may undergo a surgical procedure without consent or beyond the consent given, and that the patient must be informed and enlightened about the subject and consequences of the intervention when consent is taken. In principle, the regulation does not impose a particular form for consent, although in practice clinics use signed forms.

In a dental case the question is usually whether the patient understood beforehand what the procedure involves and what alternatives existed. Preparing a tooth for a crown or veneer removes tooth structure that cannot be restored, so the patient should have been told so and, where they exist, offered less invasive options. Missing, rushed or untranslated information can support a claim even where the technical work was acceptable. Pre-travel quotations and chat messages are evidence of what was explained.

Who can be liable

The clinic. The clinic is the contracting party in most cases. A debtor who does not perform the obligation properly must compensate the creditor unless it proves that it was not at fault (TBK Art. 112). The debtor is also answerable for the acts of the staff to whom it delegated performance (Art. 116), and an employer is liable for harm caused by employees in the course of their work unless it proves proper selection, instruction and supervision (Art. 66).

The doctor or dentist. A practitioner who causes damage by a wrongful and faulty act is liable in tort (Art. 49), and the injured person must prove the damage and the fault (Art. 50). The practitioner may be liable alongside the clinic, depending on who the patient contracted with.

The agency or facilitator. Many British patients book through an intermediary. An agency that only introduced the patient to the clinic is in a different position from one that sold a treatment package, took the payment and promised the result. A person who holds itself out as providing a service for a fee can itself be a service provider under Law No. 6502. The intermediary's own authorisation status under the health tourism rules is covered in the hub article. Payment receipts, invoices and the identity of the contracting company are the first documents to check.

What can be claimed

  • Refund or price reduction. Where the contract is treated as one for a result, the patient may rely on the rights for defective work: withdrawal, price reduction or free repair (Art. 475), and the right to claim damages under the general rules is preserved.
  • Pecuniary damages. Bodily harm covers treatment costs, loss of earnings, loss of working capacity and loss of economic future (TBK Art. 54). Corrective treatment in the UK, travel and time off work may fall within this, provided they can be shown.
  • Non-pecuniary damages. A judge may award an appropriate sum for harm to bodily integrity (Art. 56) or to personality rights (Art. 58).

Consumer rules on defective services. Where the patient is a consumer, Law No. 6502 adds its own remedies. A service is defective if it does not have the qualities agreed or objectively expected, or the features announced by the provider, including on its website or in its advertising (Art. 13). The provider must perform the service in line with the contract (Art. 14). For a defective service the consumer may choose to have the service performed again, to have the resulting work repaired free of charge, a price reduction in proportion to the defect, or to withdraw from the contract, and may claim damages under the Code of Obligations in addition (Art. 15). A clinic's own promises in its quotation, website or messages can therefore matter for what counts as a defect.

The court sets amounts on the evidence, so every expense should be documented.

Evidence and the court-appointed expert

Turkish courts decide medical questions with the help of a court-appointed expert (bilirkişi), whenever the matter needs technical knowledge outside the law (Code of Civil Procedure, Art. 266). In medical disputes, courts often ask medical bodies, including the Forensic Medicine Institution (Adli Tıp Kurumu), for a report.

Useful evidence includes:

  • the contract, quotation, treatment plan and consent forms;
  • X-rays, scans and photographs before, during and after treatment;
  • all payment proofs and the name of the company that received the money;
  • messages with the clinic and the agency, including promises about results;
  • a report from an independent dentist or doctor in the UK describing the current condition and the corrective treatment required, with a written costing.

Consumer arbitration committee, mediation and the consumer court

Disputes below the threshold. For 2026, the monetary limit is 186,000 TL, adjusted each January (Law No. 6502 Art. 68(4) and the Ministry of Trade communiqué published on 23 December 2025). Below the limit, an application to the consumer arbitration committee is mandatory. Above it, an application to the committee is not possible. The application can be made to the committee where the consumer lives or where the transaction took place (Art. 68(3)). The committee's decision binds the parties, with an objection to the consumer court possible within two weeks of notification, and the committee cannot award attorney's fees (Art. 70).

Disputes at or above the threshold. Before a consumer lawsuit can be filed, applying to a mediator is a condition of the lawsuit (Art. 73/A(1)). Disputes within the arbitration committee's competence are excluded from this condition. The consumer's mediation fee is met from the Ministry of Justice budget, up to a cap set in the law (Art. 73/A(3)). If mediation fails, the consumer court (tüketici mahkemesi) has jurisdiction (Art. 73(1)).

A claim can be run from the UK by power of attorney; see the hub article.

Time limits

Each legal basis has its own period.

  • Tort. A damages claim is time-barred two years after the injured person learns of the damage and the person liable, and in any case ten years after the act. A longer criminal limitation period applies if the act is also a criminal offence and the criminal period is longer (TBK Art. 72).
  • General contract claims. Ten years (Art. 146).
  • Claims arising from a mandate. Five years (Art. 147(5)). Whether this covers a claim for damages for breach, as well as claims for fees, is debated.
  • Defective work. Claims for defects in a work that is not a building are time-barred two years after delivery, or twenty years if the contractor was grossly at fault (Art. 478). The obligation to inspect and report defects promptly also applies (Arts. 474 and 477).
  • Defective service to a consumer. Two years from the performance of the service, even if the defect appears later, unless a longer period applies by law or contract. The period does not apply if the defect was concealed through gross fault or fraud (Law No. 6502, Art. 16).
  • The Code of Obligations periods cannot be changed by contract (Art. 148).

Compulsory malpractice insurance

Doctors and dentists working freelance or in public or private health institutions must hold compulsory medical malpractice liability insurance. The legal basis is Additional Art. 12 of Law No. 1219, as applied in the official General Conditions. Private institutions insure each practitioner separately, and cover applies to professional activity performed within Turkey. It covers compensation claims made against the practitioner, with related legal costs, within the limits of the policy. It is a source of payment for a judgment against the practitioner and does not replace proof of fault or damage.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sue a dentist in Turkey from the UK?

Yes. A claim can be brought in the Turkish courts without travelling for every step. A lawyer in Turkey can act under a power of attorney. The patient may still be needed for a medical examination ordered during the case.

Do I have to go to mediation before suing a Turkish clinic?

For a consumer dispute above the arbitration committee threshold, yes. Applying to a mediator is a condition of a consumer lawsuit (Law No. 6502 Art. 73/A). Below the threshold, the application goes to the consumer arbitration committee instead.

What is the consumer arbitration committee limit in 2026?

186,000 TL. Disputes below that value must go to the committee, and the limit is updated each January.

Is a patient treated in Turkey a consumer under Turkish law?

The Consumer Protection Act defines a consumer as a person acting for non-commercial purposes and covers mandate and work contracts, with no nationality requirement. A private patient is therefore generally within the Act.

Who is liable if I booked through an agency?

An agency that sold the package, took the payment or promised the result can be liable alongside the clinic. The clinic is usually the contracting party for treatment. An agency that sold the package, took the payment or promised the result may have its own liability. Receipts and the identity of the company that was paid decide the question.

How long do I have to bring a claim?

Two years from knowledge for a tort claim (with a ten-year outer limit), five or ten years for contract claims and two years from delivery for defective work.

Does the clinic's insurance pay my compensation?

Doctors and dentists must carry compulsory malpractice insurance, which responds to claims against the practitioner within the policy limits. It pays where fault and damage are established, within the policy terms.
