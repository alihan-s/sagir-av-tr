Property disputes in Turkey for foreign buyers: your options

Dr Alihan Sağır, Turkish lawyer (Avukat), Antalya Bar Association · Prepared: 12 November 2026

Many foreign buyers on the Antalya and Alanya coast pay a deposit or the full price and then face a late building, a refused refund, a defect or a missing title deed. The legal options depend on the form of the contract, who the seller is and what has already been registered. This guide explains the main routes under Turkish law in plain English, including mediation and acting from abroad.

In short

  1. The form of the contract decides a lot. A sale of real estate, and a promise to sell, are valid only if made in official form: a title deed transfer at the land registry, or a sale promise before a notary (Code of Obligations, Art. 237; Civil Code, Art. 706). A private "reservation" or "sales agreement" signed at a sales office is not enough on its own.
  2. A void contract does not mean the money is lost. Money paid without a valid legal basis can be claimed back as unjust enrichment (Code of Obligations, Art. 77), but the claim is time-barred two years after the buyer learns of it (Art. 82).
  3. Off-plan purchases from a developer are often consumer sales. The Consumer Protection Act gives the buyer specific rights, including a delivery limit of 48 months and a right to withdraw (Arts. 44 and 45).
  4. Defects must be reported promptly. The remedies and time limits differ depending on whether the seller is a business or a private person.
  5. If the title deed is wrong or not transferred, a court action to correct the register (tapu iptal ve tescil) may be possible, and a court can order a precautionary measure to protect the claim while the case runs.
  6. The route depends on the dispute: consumer arbitration committee, consumer court or general civil court. Some disputes require mediation before a lawsuit can be filed.
  7. You can act from abroad with a power of attorney, but the documents must be right.
  8. Keep evidence now: contract, bank transfer receipts, messages, permits and title deed records.

Why the form of the contract matters

In Turkey, a contract to sell real estate is valid only when it is made in official form (Code of Obligations, Art. 237). The same applies to a promise to sell: it is valid only if made in official form, normally a gayrimenkul satış vaadi sözleşmesi signed before a notary. A promise to make a contract in the future is valid, but its validity depends on the form of the contract it promises (Art. 29).

In practice many buyers sign only a reservation form or a private sales agreement, often drawn up by the agency or the developer's sales office. Such a paper may be formally invalid. That does not by itself mean the buyer has no claim. Money paid under a contract that turns out to be invalid is an enrichment of the seller without a valid basis, and the Code of Obligations (Art. 77) obliges the seller to return it. The right to claim back an unjust enrichment becomes time-barred two years after the buyer learns of the right and in any case ten years after the enrichment (Art. 82), so waiting is a risk.

For off-plan sales to consumers, the Consumer Protection Act (No. 6502) goes further. If the formalities were not followed (registration at the land registry for the sale, a notarised sale promise), the seller cannot later rely on the invalidity of the contract against the consumer (Art. 41(1)). The seller also may not ask for any payment before a valid contract exists (Art. 41(2)).

Off-plan and pre-paid sales: delays and refunds

A "pre-paid housing sale" (ön ödemeli konut satışı) is one in which the consumer pays the price before or during construction and the seller undertakes to transfer or deliver the home after payment (Consumer Protection Act, Art. 40). Whether the Act applies depends on the facts, in particular whether the buyer acts as a consumer and the seller as a business. Nationality does not matter: a foreign buyer can be a consumer under Turkish law in the same way as a Turkish buyer.

Where it applies, the Act provides, among other things:

  • The seller must not contract with consumers before obtaining the construction licence, and must give a pre-contract information form at least one day before the contract (Art. 40).
  • The consumer can withdraw within 14 days without giving a reason (Art. 43).
  • The home must be delivered within the period stated in the contract, and in any case within 48 months of the contract date (Art. 44).
  • The consumer may withdraw from the contract without giving a reason for up to 24 months from the contract date. In that case the seller may claim the tax and registration costs and a capped compensation: up to 2% of the contract price in the first three months, rising to 8% between 12 and 24 months (Art. 45(1)).
  • If the seller does not perform, or performs improperly, the seller cannot claim any amount from the consumer (Art. 45(2)).
  • After withdrawal, the amount owed to the consumer must be returned within 180 days at the latest from the date the notice reaches the seller (Art. 45(3)).

For projects with thirty or more homes, the seller must, before starting sales, take out building-completion insurance or provide one of the other securities set by the regulation: a bank letter of guarantee, a progress-payment system or a linked loan (Act, Art. 42; Regulation on Pre-Paid Housing Sales, Art. 12). Under a bank letter of guarantee, buyers can claim what they paid from the bank if the seller does not deliver the home as required (Regulation, Art. 14). Ask the developer in writing which security exists, and keep the document.

The 24-month right in Art. 45 runs from the contract date and does not depend on any fault of the seller. It is separate from the buyer's rights when the seller is in default: those rights remain whether or not the Act applies and whether or not 24 months have passed. Under the general rules of the Code of Obligations on delay, after a reasonable period given to the seller to perform (or without one in the cases listed in Art. 124), the buyer may demand performance and damages for the delay, or give up performance and claim damages, or withdraw from the contract and ask for what was paid back (Arts. 123–125). Which route fits depends on the contract and the facts.

Practical points: send written notices that can be proved, keep the date and content, and take advice before sending a withdrawal notice, because the choice of right affects the others.

Defects after handover

For a home sold by a business to a consumer, the buyer may choose one of four remedies (Consumer Protection Act, Art. 11): withdraw from the contract, keep the home and ask for a price reduction in proportion to the defect, ask for free repair if that does not cost disproportionately much, or ask for replacement where possible. Repair or replacement must be done within 60 working days for homes and holiday properties (Art. 11(4)). Compensation under the Code of Obligations can be claimed in addition (Art. 11(6)).

The seller's liability for a defect in a home is time-barred five years from delivery (at least three years for a second-hand home sold by a business, Art. 12(2)), and the time bar does not apply if the defect was concealed through gross negligence or fraud (Art. 12). A defect that appears within six months of delivery is presumed to have existed at delivery (Art. 10).

If the seller is a private person, the Code of Obligations applies. The buyer must inspect the home as soon as the ordinary course of things allows and notify the seller within a reasonable time; a buyer who fails to do so is treated as having accepted the home, except for a hidden defect, which must be reported immediately once discovered (Art. 223). Actions for defects in a building are time-barred five years after ownership passes, or twenty years if the seller was grossly at fault (Art. 244). Report defects in writing and keep photographs, expert reports and invoices.

Title deed problems

Ownership of real estate is acquired by registration in the land register (Civil Code, Art. 705). Typical problems are:

  • Paid, but no transfer. If the seller refuses to appear at the land registry, the buyer's claim depends on the contract. With a valid notarised sale promise, a court can be asked to transfer ownership by judgment (Civil Code, Art. 716). Without one, the buyer will usually be limited to claiming back the money and damages.
  • The seller sold to someone else. If the second buyer has already been registered, the buyer's claim is normally against the seller for a refund and damages. A claim against the second buyer may be possible only in limited cases, for example where that buyer knowingly acted together with the seller. If the buyer's notarised sale promise was annotated on the title deed before the second sale, it can be asserted against the later purchaser (Civil Code, Art. 1009).
  • A mortgage or other annotation appears later. The register shows mortgages, seizures and other annotations. Once a mortgage or seizure is registered, removing it usually means a dispute with the seller's creditor as well as the seller. The cheaper course is to have a lawyer check the title deed record before any money is paid, and to pay against the transfer at the land registry (see Buying property in Turkey as a foreigner).
  • An unlawful registration. A person whose ownership or other right in rem is harmed by a registration made without a valid basis can sue to have the register corrected (Civil Code, Art. 1025). A buyer with a valid sale contract or notarised sale promise whose seller refuses to transfer can ask the court to transfer ownership by judgment (Civil Code, Art. 716). Both are commonly called tapu iptal ve tescil. The rights of good-faith third parties and any claim for damages are preserved. Cases about rights in real estate must be filed at the court where the property is located, which has exclusive jurisdiction (Code of Civil Procedure, Art. 12). Where the buyer is a consumer and the seller a developer, the case is heard by the consumer court of the place where the property is located.

Protecting the claim while the case runs. A sale promise can be annotated on the title deed (Civil Code, Art. 1009), and the annotation can be raised against those who acquire rights in the property afterwards. The annotation lapses after five years (Land Registry Act, Art. 26); the claim under the sale promise itself is time-barred after ten years, so an annotated buyer should not wait for the annotation to expire. A court can also order an interim measure (ihtiyati tedbir) when there is a concern that the right will become much harder or impossible to obtain, or serious harm will result from delay (Code of Civil Procedure, Art. 389). The applicant must show their claim is probably well founded (Art. 390) and normally must give security (Art. 392). If the measure is ordered before the lawsuit is filed, the lawsuit must be filed within two weeks of asking for the measure to be enforced, or the measure lapses (Art. 397). Where mediation is required, this period does not run while mediation is in progress (Law No. 6325, Art. 18/A(16)). A court may also order a provisional registration annotation to secure an alleged right in rem (Civil Code, Art. 1011). Whether any of these is available depends on the facts.

Fraud can also be reported to the public prosecutor. The criminal complaint and the civil claim are separate.

Which route: committee, court or mediation

The right forum depends on who the parties are and what is claimed. The Ministry of Trade oversees the consumer arbitration committees (tüketici hakem heyeti).

  • Consumer arbitration committee. For consumer disputes below a monetary limit, an application to the committee is compulsory and a lawsuit cannot be filed directly; above the limit the committee cannot be used (Consumer Protection Act, Art. 68). For 2026 the limit is TRY 186,000. It is updated every year. A committee decision can be challenged within two weeks of notification (Art. 70).
  • Consumer court. Disputes above the limit go to the consumer court (Art. 73). Before most consumer court cases, including claims for a refund or damages, applying to a mediator is a precondition (Art. 73/A). Mediation is not required for consumer disputes arising from rights in rem over immovable property, such as an action for annulment and registration of the title deed, nor for objections to committee decisions.
  • General civil court. A dispute with a private seller, or one that is not a consumer transaction, goes to the ordinary civil court, subject to the exclusive jurisdiction rule for real estate (Code of Civil Procedure, Art. 12).
  • Mandatory mediation. The Ministry of Justice runs the mediation system. Mediation is a precondition for lawsuits in specific categories: commercial cases for a sum of money (Commercial Code, Art. 5/A), consumer court cases (Art. 73/A above) and, since 2023, disputes about the division of property and termination of co-ownership, disputes under the Condominium Act, neighbour law, and rental disputes (Law No. 6325, Art. 18/B). A lawsuit filed without required mediation is dismissed on procedural grounds (Art. 18/A). The time spent in mediation does not count against limitation and forfeiture periods (Art. 18/A(15)).

Because the lists are specific and the exceptions are narrow, identify the route before filing. Rental disputes are a separate subject and are not covered here.

Doing it from abroad

You do not have to travel to Turkey to bring a claim. A power of attorney (vekaletname) can be signed at a Turkish consulate, or before a notary in your own country with an apostille and a sworn Turkish translation certified by a Turkish notary. A lawyer in Turkey can supply the wording so that it covers the specific acts needed, such as sending notices, mediation, filing and conducting the case, and dealing with the land registry. See Inherited property in Turkey for how the same document route works in another setting. The service page on property purchase in Antalya describes the office's work in this area.

Evidence to keep

  • The contract in every version, with any annexes, plans and the specification list.
  • Bank transfer receipts and any foreign exchange documents. Cash payments are hard to prove.
  • Messages, emails and chat records with the agent and the developer, including promised delivery dates.
  • The construction licence and, for completed buildings, the occupancy permit (yapı kullanma izin belgesi, commonly called iskan).
  • Current and past title deed records, and photographs of the building's state with dates.
  • Your own timeline: when you paid, when you complained and when the seller answered.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get my money back if my off-plan apartment in Turkey is not delivered?

Often yes, but the route depends on the contract. If the Consumer Protection Act applies, the home must be delivered within the contract period and no later than 48 months from the contract (Art. 44), and the buyer has a right to withdraw (Art. 45). If it does not apply, the rules on delay in the Code of Obligations apply. Even if the contract is formally invalid, the money can be claimed back as unjust enrichment.

Is a reservation agreement from an estate agent binding in Turkey?

A reservation or private sales agreement is not valid as a sale of real estate, which requires official form. In a consumer off-plan sale, however, the developer cannot rely on the missing form against the buyer (Consumer Protection Act, Art. 41). The agreement can still matter as evidence of the payment and of what was agreed, and a refund claim may be possible.

How long do I have to complain about a defect?

It depends on the seller. For a consumer sale of a home by a business, liability lasts five years from delivery (Consumer Protection Act, Art. 12). For a private seller, the buyer must notify defects promptly, and actions for building defects are time-barred five years after ownership passes (Code of Obligations, Arts. 223 and 244). Report defects in writing without delay.

The developer sold my flat twice. What can I do?

Check the title deed record first. If the other buyer is already registered, your claim is normally against the seller for the refund and damages; a claim against the other buyer is possible only in limited cases, for example if that buyer knowingly acted together with the seller. If you have a notarised sale promise annotated on the deed, your position is stronger. Take advice quickly, because an interim measure may be possible only while the property is still registered to the seller.

Do I need mediation before suing?

For money claims, usually yes. A refund or damages claim against a developer in the consumer court requires mediation first, and so do commercial money claims and certain property-related disputes such as co-ownership, condominium and rental cases. An action for annulment and registration of the title deed does not require mediation. Below the 2026 limit of TRY 186,000, a consumer claim goes to the consumer arbitration committee instead.

Can I bring a claim without being in Turkey?

Yes. A power of attorney signed at a Turkish consulate, or before a notary abroad with an apostille and a sworn Turkish translation certified by a Turkish notary, allows a lawyer in Turkey to act for you. Evidence such as bank receipts and messages can be sent electronically.

What should I do before buying, to avoid a dispute later?

Have a lawyer check the property before you pay anything. The checks cover the title deed record (owner, mortgages, seizures, annotations), the municipality's file (construction licence, occupancy permit, approved plans) and the property itself compared with those documents. Building in breach of the licence, or changes made after the occupancy permit, are common: a flat enlarged by taking in part of a common area or a balcony is a typical example. Such breaches can lead to demolition orders, which stay with the building whoever owns it, and the new owner may face fines and the cost of putting things right. Since 2020, municipalities must also notify a breach to the land registry, where it is noted on the title deed record (Zoning Act No. 3194, Arts. 32 and 42). Finding them before the contract usually costs less than a lawsuit afterwards. See Buying property in Turkey as a foreigner for the full checklist.
